Reviewer 2 Response: Phosphine Gas in the Cloud Decks of Venus

Target paper: Greaves et al., Nature Astronomy (2020) · DOI: 10.1038/s41550-020-1174-4

Abstract Under Review

"Here we report the apparent presence of phosphine (PH₃) in the atmosphere of Venus. In the temperate Venusian atmosphere (~53–61 km altitude), we detected a spectral feature at 266.94 GHz using both the James Clerk Maxwell Telescope (JCMT) and the Atacama Large Millimeter Array (ALMA). We were unable to identify any chemical process that could produce phosphine in such abundances (~20 ppb) aside from life. We conclude that the production of phosphine by life is the most plausible hypothesis." — Greaves et al., Nature Astronomy, 2020

Reviewer 2 — Summary Decision

Major Revisions Required

This manuscript makes an extraordinary claim — the presence of a biosignature in the atmosphere of a neighbouring planet — that demands an extraordinary standard of evidence. While the detection is scientifically interesting, the current manuscript contains significant unresolved issues in spectral identification, abundance estimation, and the exclusion of abiotic production pathways. I recommend major revisions before the claim of biological origin can be considered credible.

Weakness 1

Spectral Line Identification and Calibration Artefacts

Issue: The claimed PH₃ detection rests on a single spectral line at 266.94 GHz. This frequency overlaps with a known SO₂ absorption feature at 267.54 GHz and is within the instrumental calibration uncertainty of the ALMA dataset. Critically, the ALMA data were subsequently retracted by the observatory (NRAO, October 2020) due to a calibration error that artificially inflated signal amplitudes by a factor of ~2. The reprocessed ALMA data yielded a far weaker residual signal that is no longer statistically robust without independent confirmation.

Suggested improvement: The authors must provide updated figures using the corrected ALMA pipeline (CASA v5.7+) and clearly quantify the residual line strength post-correction. A spectral decomposition explicitly modelling the SO₂ contribution and its uncertainty envelope must be included. The detection claim should be suspended until independent confirmation is obtained from a third observatory (e.g., IRAM 30m or VLA).

Weakness 2

Phosphine Abundance Significantly Overstated

Issue: The reported abundance of ~20 ppb is the central pillar of the authors' argument that abiotic processes cannot explain the signal. However, this figure derives from the uncorrected ALMA dataset. Independent reanalyses (Snellen et al., 2020; Villanueva et al., 2020) using the same raw JCMT data recover an upper bound of <1–7 ppb — a reduction of at least 3×. At sub-7 ppb levels, known abiotic sources (volcanic degassing, lightning-induced chemistry, photochemical pathways) cannot be confidently ruled out with current Venusian atmospheric models.

Suggested improvement: The paper must be revised with the corrected abundance estimate as the headline figure. The entire section on "production efficiency required from abiotic sources" must be recalculated accordingly. A formal sensitivity analysis showing at what abundance threshold abiotic explanations become implausible is required.

Weakness 3

Abiotic Production Pathways Insufficiently Excluded

Issue: The manuscript dismisses abiotic PH₃ sources with qualitative arguments and rough upper-bound estimates for volcanic and lightning production. However, Venus's interior geochemistry is poorly constrained: (i) reducing volcanism involving phosphide-bearing magmas has not been observationally excluded; (ii) subsurface-to-atmosphere outgassing transport timescales are not modelled; (iii) recent work on UV photochemical reduction of phosphates in the cloud layer (Bains et al., 2020, rebuttal) demonstrates conditions under which non-biological pathways could produce ppb-level PH₃. The "life is the only remaining explanation" conclusion is therefore logically premature.

Suggested improvement: A dedicated supplementary section must quantitatively model each abiotic pathway with propagated uncertainties. The authors should collaborate with a Venus atmospheric chemistry specialist to bound volcanic PH₃ flux. The conclusion must be rephrased to reflect the actual epistemic status: "we cannot currently identify a confirmed abiotic source" — not "life is the most plausible hypothesis."

Minor Points

  • Temporal variability — Both JCMT and ALMA observations are separated by months. PH₃ abundance variability with season or atmospheric dynamics is not discussed. A temporally variable abiotic source cannot be distinguished from a biological one with two snapshots.
  • Altitude profile — The claim is for a broad 53–61 km layer, yet the spectral retrieval does not constrain the vertical distribution. A model that places all PH₃ at 55 km vs. 60 km produces different photochemical destruction rates and therefore different production rate requirements.
  • Statistical presentation — The reported significance (~15σ for JCMT, ~11σ for ALMA) is prominently displayed but computed against thermal noise only. Line confusion, baseline ripple, and systematic calibration offsets are not included in the quoted significance. The Bayesian evidence for the PH₃ model vs. the null (SO₂ + noise) model is not computed.

Reviewer 2 — Recommendation

I commend the authors for pursuing this bold detection and for the transparency of making their data available. However, the manuscript as submitted cannot sustain its primary conclusion. The compound effect of the calibration error, the abundance revision, and the incomplete exclusion of abiotic sources means that the extraordinary claim of a biological origin is not yet supported by the evidence. I encourage the authors to revise thoroughly — a corrected, more cautious paper remains highly publishable and scientifically significant, even without the Life hypothesis.
